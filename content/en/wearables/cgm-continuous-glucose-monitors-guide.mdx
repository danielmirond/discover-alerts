A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) measures your interstitial glucose every 1-5 minutes, 24 hours a day, for 14 days. No finger pricks, no test strips. A coin-sized sensor stuck to your arm that sends data to your phone in real time.

Until 2020, CGMs were exclusively for diabetics. In 2026, they are the most adopted metabolic wearable in the longevity community. The reason: postprandial glucose is an independent predictor of cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline and mortality. and most "healthy" people have spikes they would never detect with a fasting blood test.

<EvidenceBadge level={3} label="Strong evidence" />

## Why glucose matters for longevity

### The problem with fasting glucose

Your doctor measures fasting glucose once a year. If it's between 70-100 mg/dL, they say "normal." But that number tells you nothing about what happens after eating, during exercise, during stress or during the night.

A study by [Hall et al., 2018 (PLOS Biology)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30040822/) monitored 57 "healthy" people (non-diabetic, normal fasting glucose) with CGM for 2-4 weeks. Discovery: 96% had glucose spikes above 140 mg/dL, and 15% spent more than 5% of their time above that threshold. Levels that a one-time blood test would never detect.

### What the evidence says

**[Levitan et al., 2004 (Archives of Internal Medicine)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15505128/):** Meta-analysis of 38 prospective studies. Elevated postprandial glucose (>140 mg/dL) is an independent predictor of cardiovascular disease, even in people without diabetes. Each 1 mmol/L increase in post-meal glucose increases cardiovascular risk by 11%.

**[Crane et al., 2013 (New England Journal of Medicine)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23924004/):** Cohort study in 2,067 adults without dementia. Higher glucose levels (even within the "normal" range) were associated with higher risk of dementia. Average glucose of 115 mg/dL vs 100 mg/dL already increased risk by 18%.

**[DECODE Study Group, 2001 (The Lancet)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11179958/):** 2-hour post-load glucose (tolerance test) predicts all-cause mortality better than fasting glucose. People with "normal" fasting but high postprandial glucose have significantly elevated cardiovascular risk.

### The four metrics that matter

| Metric | Optimal (longevity) | Medical normal | Problematic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **24h average glucose** | 80-95 mg/dL | <100 | >100 |
| **Postprandial peak** | <120 mg/dL | <140 | >160 |
| **Variability (SD)** | <15 mg/dL | <20 | >25 |
| **Time in range (70-120)** | >90% | >70% | <70% |

## How a CGM works

### The sensor

A flexible 5mm filament is inserted under the skin (subcutaneous) on the back of the arm. It's painless. the applicator places it in one second. The filament measures glucose in interstitial fluid (not blood directly), which has a 5-15 minute delay relative to blood glucose.

### The transmission

The sensor transmits data via Bluetooth or NFC to the phone. Frequency: every 1 minute (Dexcom G7) or per scan (Libre 2) or continuous (Libre 3).

### Duration

- **Abbott Libre 3:** 14 days
- **Dexcom G7:** 10 days
- **Dexcom Stelo:** 15 days (new, no prescription needed in the US)

## What you'll learn with a CGM

### Week 1: initial discoveries

The first week with a CGM is revelatory. You'll discover things a blood test would never show you:

- **Your favorite breakfast is probably a spike.** Toast with jam, cereal, orange juice. most Western breakfasts trigger peaks of 160-200 mg/dL
- **Food order matters.** Eating salad before rice reduces the postprandial spike by 30-40% (Shukla et al., 2017)
- **Stress raises glucose without eating.** A stressful meeting can elevate glucose 20-30 mg/dL via cortisol
- **Sleep deprivation destroys your glycemic response.** One night of 5 hours worsens next-day insulin sensitivity by 20-30%
- **Post-meal exercise is the best "drug."** 10 minutes of walking after eating reduces the spike by 30%

### Weeks 2-4: optimization

With the first week's data, you start optimizing:

1. **Identify your problem foods.** Each person responds differently to the same foods (Zeevi et al., 2015. Cell). Your bread might be your poison
2. **Find your exercise window.** When exercise has the most impact on your glucose (generally 30-60 min post-meal)
3. **Calibrate your macros.** What protein/fat/fiber ratio you need to add to carbs to flatten the spike
4. **Measure the sleep effect.** Correlate sleep quality (Oura) with next-day glycemic response

## The 7 golden rules for stable glucose

### 1. Vinegar before eating

1-2 tablespoons of apple cider vinegar in water 15 minutes before a carb-heavy meal reduces the postprandial spike by 20-30%. Acetic acid slows gastric emptying and improves muscular glucose uptake.

**[Johnston et al., 2004 (Diabetes Care)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14694010/):** Vinegar before a high-carb meal improved insulin sensitivity by 34% in people with insulin resistance.

### 2. Fiber/protein/fat before carbs

The order you eat matters more than what you eat. The optimal sequence: vegetables and protein first, carbs last. Fiber and fat slow glucose absorption.

### 3. Walk 10 minutes after eating

Muscle contraction activates GLUT4 (insulin-independent glucose transporters). 10 minutes of gentle walking after eating is as effective as metformin for reducing spikes in healthy people.

### 4. Don't eat carbs alone

A banana alone: 40+ mg/dL spike. A banana with peanut butter: 15 mg/dL spike. Fat and protein slow digestion and buffer the peak.

### 5. Sleep 7-8 hours

Sleep deprivation is the silent destroyer of insulin sensitivity. One night of 4-5 hours can worsen your glycemic response by 40% the next day (Spiegel et al., 1999).

### 6. Manage stress

Cortisol elevates glucose via hepatic gluconeogenesis. Chronic stress keeps baseline glucose elevated without eating anything. Resonance breathing, meditation, nature.

### 7. Resistance training

Muscle is the body's largest glucose sink. More muscle mass = more capacity to store glucose as glycogen = lower spikes. Resistance training improves insulin sensitivity for 24-48 hours post-session.

## Who should use a CGM

### Makes sense for

- **People with prediabetes** (HbA1c 5.7-6.4% or fasting glucose 100-125). the CGM shows you exactly what's spiking you
- **Metabolic biohackers.** you want to optimize energy, cognition and body composition
- **People with unexplained post-meal fatigue.** often caused by glucose spikes and crashes
- **Athletes.** optimize fueling before, during and after exercise
- **People with family history of type 2 diabetes.** active prevention

### You probably don't need one if

- You're <30 years old, active, sleeping well and eating varied
- Your HbA1c is <5.2% and you have no metabolic symptoms
- You already know what to eat and are seeking confirmation (use 1 month and stop)
- Your budget is very tight ($150/month isn't trivial)

## The 1-month protocol

You don't need a CGM forever. 1 month is enough to learn what you need:

| Week | Objective | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Baseline | Eat as usual. Observe without changing anything |
| **2** | Experiments | Test: food order, vinegar, post-meal walk |
| **3** | Optimization | Apply what you learned. Identify your 5 optimal meals |
| **4** | Confirmation | Verify that your changes keep glucose in range |

After the month, you know what foods spike you, what habits help and what combinations work. You don't need to keep measuring.
